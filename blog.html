The UK–India CETA lands on 15 July 2026. Here's what North India manufacturers must do now.

Kinexus SystemsJune 20266 min read

A duty advantage is not a strategy. It's an open door — and doors close. The manufacturers who convert the FTA into real orders will be the ones who get operationally export-ready while the advantage still exists.

From 15 July 2026, the India–UK Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement (CETA) eliminates UK duties on 99% of Indian tariff lines — cutting tariffs of up to 18% on engineering goods and auto components, 12% on textiles and clothing, and 8% on chemicals and pharmaceuticals. On paper, an Indian factory just became materially cheaper to buy from than many global competitors. The EU–India FTA, whose negotiations concluded in January 2026 (with entry into force expected around 2027), opens an even larger market close behind.

But here is the uncomfortable truth every exporter learns: price gets you onto the shortlist; operations win the order. A serious overseas buyer doesn't just compare quotes. They audit. They ask for batch traceability, consistent quality records, documentation that survives their customs, and the confidence that you'll hit delivery dates shipment after shipment. That's where most first-time exporters stall.

The 12-month window, in three moves

1. Make your operation visible (months 0–3). You cannot pass a buyer audit on memory and spreadsheets. The first move is a single source of truth: production, quality and inventory captured in one place, in real time. This isn't a giant ERP project — it's a focused backbone built on what you already use.

2. Build export-grade traceability and documentation (months 3–6). Batch-level traceability is the single capability that most often separates "we'd struggle to reconstruct that" from "here's the full record in one click." Pair it with a compliance register per product and destination, and your documentation stops being a last-minute scramble.

3. Win and service the first buyer (months 6–12). Faster quotations, a pipeline that holds every enquiry, and a credible, visible operation behind the offer. The goal isn't one lucky shipment — it's a repeatable export motion.

Here's the part nobody mentions: only about 28% of eligible Indian exports actually claim their FTA benefit — the rules-of-origin paperwork costs more than the duty it saves. The tariff advantage is shared by everyone; the lasting edge belongs to whoever becomes operationally export-ready first.

Don't wait for the order to get ready

The most common mistake is treating export-readiness as something you sort out after a buyer appears. By then it's too late — the buyer has moved on. Readiness is the thing that attracts the buyer in the first place. Start now, while the duty advantage is buying you attention you'd otherwise have to earn on price alone.

"Not export-ready" really means "not digitised yet."

Kinexus SystemsJune 20264 min read

Ask any export consultant why a manufacturer's deal stalled and you'll hear the same phrase: "they weren't export-ready." Look closely at what that actually means on the floor, and almost every reason is a data problem in disguise.

When engagements break down, the causes cluster into a familiar list: documentation and compliance gaps, no batch traceability, quality that varies between batches, no real-time visibility, and decisions made on gut feel. Each of these sounds like a different problem. They're not. They're all symptoms of the same root cause — an operation that isn't digitised.

Trace each "readiness" gap back to its source

  • Can't pass a buyer's traceability audit? Because batch data lives in someone's head or a paper register, not a system.
  • Quality varies between batches? Because inspections aren't captured and analysed — so problems are caught at the buyer's end, not yours.
  • Documentation always last-minute and error-prone? Because it's re-typed by hand each time instead of generated from your records.
  • Can't commit confidently to delivery dates? Because there's no live view of production versus plan.
Digitisation isn't a luxury you add after you've become an exporter. It's the foundation that makes you one.

Why this reframe matters

If "not export-ready" is really "not digitised," then the fix isn't more advice — it's building the operational backbone that turns those gaps into strengths. A manufacturer who can show a buyer real traceability, consistent quality data, and clean documentation hasn't just ticked boxes. They've become the supplier the buyer trusts.

That's the whole logic of the Digital Export Engine: digitise first, and export-readiness follows as a consequence — not the other way around.

Ayurveda & wellness exports: a first-timer's compliance map.

Kinexus SystemsJune 20265 min read

Global demand for Ayurveda, herbal and natural-wellness products is rising faster than almost any category India makes — and India's share of it is tiny. The gap isn't demand or product. It's the compliance maze that sinks first-time exporters before they ship.

Wellness is the widest "potential-minus-penetration" gap in Indian exports, which is exactly why it's such an opportunity. But it's also one of the most compliance-sensitive categories, because the product goes into or onto a human body. Get the classification, labelling or claims wrong, and your shipment is held — or rejected.

The four traps that cause most rejections

  • The medicine-vs-food classification fork. Is your product a medicament or a food supplement? The destination regulator decides, and it changes everything downstream — the HS code, the documentation, the permitted claims. This single call is the highest-leverage decision in a wellness export.
  • Unsupported health claims. What's normal marketing language in India can be an illegal claim abroad (especially under US FDA / DSHEA rules). "Cures," "treats," and even some "boosts" language can stop a shipment cold.
  • Labelling and ingredient disclosure. Destination-specific formats, allergen rules, and ingredient naming conventions trip up almost every first-timer.
  • GMP / AYUSH documentation. Buyers and regulators want to see the manufacturing and quality trail — which, again, is a traceability and documentation problem.
In wellness, the difference between a held shipment and a cleared one is usually paperwork and a classification call — not the product itself.

How to de-risk it

Two things make wellness exports far safer. First, get the classification and claims reviewed before you build the label and the listing — not after a rejection. Second, run the operation on a digital backbone so GMP/AYUSH documentation and batch traceability are generated from your records, not assembled by hand under deadline pressure.

Crawl, Walk, Run, Fly: a maturity model for export-ready manufacturing.

Kinexus SystemsJune 20264 min read

Most digital-transformation advice fails because it ignores where a factory actually is today. You can't sell autonomous AI to a plant that still runs on paper. So we map every manufacturer to one of four stages — and chart the fastest move from there.

The four stages

Crawl — paper & WhatsApp (~47% of North India MSMEs). Records live in the owner's head and a WhatsApp group. The highest-value move isn't fancy AI — it's making the invisible visible: capturing what's actually happening, with minimal disruption.

Walk — partial systems (~33%). Data exists, but in three systems no one fully trusts. The win here is unifying it into one source of truth — without ripping out the Tally or Excel you already rely on.

Run — connected data (~14%). Systems talk to each other, but people still do all the analysis. Now you're ready for an intelligence layer that surfaces insights and takes pre-approved actions.

Fly — autonomous (~3%). The destination. Export documents and compliance generated end-to-end, procurement and scheduling handled by agents within approved limits, and the owner free to be a strategist instead of a firefighter.

The point of the model isn't to label you. It's to identify the single fastest, highest-value next move — and nothing more.

Why a maturity model beats a sales pitch

A software vendor sells you the same product whatever your stage. A maturity model does the opposite: it diagnoses before it prescribes, so you never waste a sprint on the wrong fix. That's why every Kinexus engagement starts with finding your stage — and why "Fly" isn't generic autonomy, but something specific and worth wanting: a self-running export business.

What the UAE is buying from India's wellness shelf — the 2026 demand map.

Kinexus SystemsJune 20267 min read

The UAE imports the large majority of the wellness products it consumes — and India, despite owning the heritage and the manufacturing base, sends most of its wellness exports West to the US and EU, not to the Gulf next door. That mismatch, sitting on top of a duty-free trade lane India already has, is the opportunity.

Start with the scale of the demand. The Global Wellness Institute puts the UAE's wellness economy at US$40.8 billion in 2024 — the fastest-growing in the Middle East — including US$14.8 billion in personal care & beauty and US$11.3 billion in wellness tourism (GWI, 2024). The narrower "health & wellness" product market alone is put at US$19.8 billion in 2025, rising to US$28.2 billion by 2034 (IMARC, 2025). And the UAE is two markets in one: a high-spending domestic market, and Dubai's re-export hub — re-exports are over 45% of total UAE trade — that feeds the wider GCC, Africa and South Asia (Dubai Customs data, 2025).

The products Gulf buyers want — Ayurvedic and herbal supplements, natural and halal personal care, essential oils, functional foods — are exactly what India makes at scale. The UAE imports its supplements primarily from China, India and the US (MarkNtel Advisors, 2025). So India is already in the room. The question is why it isn't winning more of it.

India's AYUSH and herbal-product exports reached about US$689 million in FY 2024-25 (India's Ministry of Commerce, reported Jan 2026). But look at where they go. The tailwind India isn't fully using: the CEPA

The under-exposure is especially striking because India already has a structural advantage in the UAE. Under the India–UAE CEPA (in force since 1 May 2022), the UAE eliminated tariffs on 97% of its tariff lines — covering about 99% of India's exports to the UAE by value, with ~90% becoming duty-free immediately (India–UAE CEPA FAQs, Govt. of India). India's non-oil exports to the UAE rose to US$27.4 billion in FY 2023-24, growing ~25.6% a year since the CEPA, and total bilateral trade has since crossed US$100 billion (Govt. of India / trade data). The duty door is already open; most wellness exporters just haven't walked through it.

The gap isn't that the UAE won't buy Indian wellness products. It's that India sends most of its AYUSH exports West, under-serves the Gulf that wants them most, and under-uses a duty-free lane it already holds. That's a readiness-and-focus gap — not a demand or product one.

Why the gap stays open (it isn't demand, and it isn't product)

What India most often lacks at the factory level is the thing Gulf buyers screen for before they place an order: certified, traceable, audit-ready supply. A buyer in Dubai comparing two suppliers chooses the one who can show halal certification, heavy-metal and microbial test reports, batch traceability, and labelling that already meets GCC (and increasingly UK/EU) rules. That's a documentation and digitisation problem, not a manufacturing one — exactly the gap the Digital Export Engine is built to close.

Who's actively buying — and what for

The demand isn't abstract. It comes from a few concrete types of buyer, in the UAE and through it:

  • UAE retail & pharmacy chains — branded, certified, shelf-ready supplements and natural personal care, with clean labels and consistent supply.
  • Dubai re-export distributors — bulk and private-label volumes routed on to Saudi Arabia, Oman, Kuwait and East Africa; re-exports are over 45% of UAE trade (Dubai Customs, 2025).
  • GCC neighbours buying through the UAE — Saudi Arabia, the largest GCC wellness market, plus Oman, whose 2025 CEPA with India added the first comprehensive traditional-medicine (AYUSH) commitment in any trade pact.
  • HoReCa, spa & wellness operators — the Gulf's vast hospitality sector, a heavy buyer of essential oils, aromatherapy and natural amenities (UAE spa market ~US$1.4B in 2024, 8.5% CAGR — GMI).

Beyond the Gulf, the same Indian wellness SKUs are sought in the US (the world's largest supplement market, ~35% of India's AYUSH exports) and the EU (~19%) — and the 2025 India–UK CETA, in force 15 July 2026, removes UK duties on 99% of Indian goods. The UAE is simply the most accessible, duty-advantaged first market — which is why it's the right place to start.

Ayurveda & herbal supplements in the UAE: demand is racing ahead of supply.

Kinexus SystemsJune 20266 min read

Herbal and Ayurvedic supplements sit inside one of the Gulf's fastest-compounding markets — and India, the natural home of the category, captures only a thin slice of the UAE's demand despite holding a duty-free trade lane into it.

The Gulf's shift toward preventive, "natural-first" health is structural, not a fad. A young population, high disposable income, high awareness of lifestyle disease, and cultural openness to traditional medicine push demand toward herbal supplements, immunity and gut-health formulations, adaptogens and Ayurvedic classical products. The numbers bear it out: the GCC nutraceuticals market was US$7.5 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach US$16.1 billion by 2034, an 8.61% CAGR (IMARC, 2025). The UAE's own dietary-supplements market is put at US$512 million in 2025, rising to US$855 million by 2031, an 8.91% CAGR (TechSci Research, 2025). India is the natural home of these products — yet much of the UAE's shelf is filled by American, European and Chinese brands.

What buyers actually want to see

Gulf importers and pharmacy buyers have a checklist, and it's remarkably consistent. Meet it and you're on the shortlist; miss any line and the order quietly goes to a competitor:

Notice the pattern: almost none of this is about the product. It's about whether you can prove the product. That proof is generated from a digitised operation — or scrambled together by hand under deadline, which is why so many orders slip.

The Oman lane, and why it matters for the UAE too

The India–Oman CEPA, signed in December 2025, includes a dedicated annexe on traditional medicine — described by India's Commerce Ministry as the first comprehensive commitment on traditional medicine (AYUSH) by any country in a trade pact, extending across all modes of supply. For an Indian supplement maker, that's a proof-of-concept GCC lane — and the same readiness that unlocks Oman is exactly what UAE and Saudi buyers screen for. Build it once, sell it across the Gulf. (Note: as of mid-2026 the Oman CEPA is signed but not yet in force; the India–UAE CEPA has been in force since May 2022.)

The takeaway: the demand is here and compounding, and India already holds a duty-free lane into the UAE. The binding constraint is readiness. Solve certification, testing, traceability and classification — digitally, so it's repeatable — and the widening market becomes your runway, not someone else's.

Halal-certified herbal care & essential oils: the UAE's fastest-rising import ask.

Kinexus SystemsJune 20266 min read

Natural, halal-certified, traceable, story-rich. That's the exact profile of personal-care and aromatherapy product Gulf buyers are chasing — and it happens to be the profile India is best placed in the world to supply.

Two adjacent categories are pulling Gulf demand hard. First, natural and herbal personal care — skin, hair and body products built on Ayurvedic and botanical formulations, increasingly expected to be halal-certified. Second, essential oils and aromatherapy, fed by the Gulf's enormous spa, hospitality and home-fragrance markets — the UAE spa market alone was about US$1.4 billion in 2024, growing at an 8.5% CAGR (Global Market Insights, 2025). The standout signal is halal: the GCC halal-cosmetics market was US$7.5 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach US$26.5 billion by 2034 — a 14.67% CAGR (IMARC, 2025), among the fastest-growing consumer categories in the region. India is a leading global producer of both the botanicals and the oils, yet its share of the UAE's import demand sits well below its production strength.

The tailwind that makes this urgent: the CEPA

The real, present advantage for an Indian exporter is the India–UAE CEPA: in force since May 2022, it gives roughly 99% of India's exports to the UAE duty-free access by value — so a halal-certified Indian botanical line already lands in Dubai without the tariff drag a non-FTA competitor carries. The 2025 India–UK CETA adds a Western-market tailwind, eliminating UK duties on 99% of Indian goods from 15 July 2026 (GOV.UK). One clarification worth making, because it's widely muddled: the much-quoted "22% cosmetics tariff" under CETA is India's duty on UK-made cosmetics — a win for British exporters into India, not a UK duty on Indian goods. India's edge into both the Gulf and the UK is the CEPA duty-free lane plus the natural/halal positioning — not that 22% figure.

What separates a winning supplier here

  • Halal certification, upfront. For personal care headed into GCC retail and re-export, this is table stakes, not a nice-to-have.
  • EU Cosmetics-grade compliance. CPNP notification, an EU Responsible Person, and a clean safety dossier — meeting the EU bar makes you trivially acceptable to Gulf buyers too.
  • Defensible claims and labelling. "Natural" and "herbal" are scrutinised; unsubstantiated claims get a shipment held. Get claims reviewed before the label is printed.
  • Batch traceability and stability data. Especially for essential oils, where purity and adulteration are buyer obsessions — provable purity is the sale.
India's edge in this category is authenticity — genuinely botanical, genuinely traditional. The job is to make that authenticity provable to a Gulf buyer: certified, tested, traceable. That's the digitisation layer, and it's what turns a story into a shipment.

Across all three pieces, the conclusion is the same. The UAE wants India's wellness products; the demand is large and growing; and the gap is held open not by demand or product but by readiness. Close the readiness gap — classification, certification, testing, traceability, labelling, all generated from a digitised operation — and the share is there for the taking.
